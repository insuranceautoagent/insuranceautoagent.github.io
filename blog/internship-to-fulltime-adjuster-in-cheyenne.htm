Internship to Full-Time Adjuster in Cheyenne

Home / Blog / Blog Details

The high plains wind in Cheyenne doesn’t whisper; it tells stories. It carries the scent of sagebrush, the distant rumble of a Union Pacific train, and, increasingly, the tense anticipation of the next big storm. When I arrived here as a wide-eyed intern two years ago, clutching a degree and a desperate hope for a foot in the door, I thought I was just starting a job. I didn’t realize I was planting myself in the epicenter of a global conversation, one defined by climate change, economic uncertainty, and the relentless resilience of communities. This is the story of how the internship desk became my command post, and how Cheyenne taught me that being an adjuster today is less about assessing damage and more about navigating the human and financial tremors of our time.

The Boots-on-the-Ground University

My first week as an intern was a masterclass in humility. My tasks were a predictable blend of filing claims, fetching coffee, and listening to seasoned adjusters swap stories that sounded like legends. They spoke of "hail the size of baseballs" in Laramie County and "straight-line winds that rearranged farmsteads." It was abstract, until my first ride-along.

The First Claim: A Lesson in Layers

We drove out to a ranch north of town after a particularly violent spring thunderstorm. The insured, a third-generation rancher named Dale, met us with a stoic expression, his eyes fixed on his damaged barn roof. My mentor, Sarah, didn’t just start measuring. She asked about the calving season, about the vintage of the timber, about how the wind sounded that night. As she pointed out to me the difference between wind damage and pre-existing wear, the claim transformed. This wasn't just a roof; it was a critical piece of infrastructure for a business already grappling with fluctuating cattle prices and drought—a direct link to global supply chain pressures and agricultural volatility. My internship stopped being about paperwork in that moment. It became about understanding the layers of vulnerability: the physical, the economic, and the deeply personal.

Cheyenne: A Microcosm of Modern Risk

Cheyenne, with its cowboy heritage bumping against data centers and wind farms, is a perfect laboratory for a 21st-century adjuster. The risks here are a concentrated dose of today’s headlines.

Climate Change Isn't a Future Threat; It's Today's Claim File

The actuarial models are being rewritten in real-time. We've always had severe weather on the high plains, but the frequency and intensity are different. The "once-in-a-century" hail events seem to come every few years. Wildfire smoke from distant, massive burns now regularly tinges our sunsets, a constant reminder of interconnected environmental risk. As an intern, I cataloged these "anomalies." As a full-time adjuster, I see them as the new baseline. My job now involves explaining coverage nuances around "concurrent causation" when wind and flood team up, a complex dance that sits at the heart of climate-related insurance debates worldwide.

The Human Element in a Digital World

Another hot-button issue I lived through was the tension between automation and the human touch. During my internship, the company rolled out a new AI-driven tool for initial damage assessment from photos. The efficiency gains were massive, but so were the errors. A cracked foundation from shifting soil could be missed, or the subtle signs of wear that indicate maintenance neglect. My role evolved from just taking notes to being the bridge. I learned that technology is a formidable tool, but it cannot sit across from Dale at his kitchen table, sense his anxiety, or understand the full context of a loss. The human adjuster becomes the essential interpreter, the validator, and often, the counselor.

The Pivot: From Observing to Owning

The transition from intern to full-time adjuster wasn't marked by a single moment, but by a gradual shift in responsibility. I went from holding the clipboard to holding the conversation.

Navigating the Post-Pandemic Landscape

Just as I was being considered for a full-time role, the world grappled with global inflation and supply chain disruptions. A damaged kitchen wasn't just a matter of material and labor costs anymore. It was about the six-month wait for a specific cabinet, the 30% increase in plywood, and the shortage of skilled contractors. My estimates had to account for economic volatility I'd only read about in textbooks. I became a quasi-economist, explaining to policyholders why their rebuild cost more than their neighbor's did two years ago, navigating the delicate balance between policy limits and real-world costs. This is where the internship's grind paid off—having seen hundreds of claims, I could now spot trends and build more robust, realistic assessments.

Cybersecurity: The Invisible Peril

Even in Cheyenne, the digital world imposes its risks. A local small business, a client of ours, was hit by a ransomware attack. Suddenly, my world of hailstones and fire had to expand to include data breaches and business interruption due to cyber events. Understanding the nuances of cyber endorsements and the interplay with traditional policies became crucial. It was a stark lesson that modern adjusting requires a mind flexible enough to jump from assessing physical roof granules to understanding the contours of a digital threat.

Rooted in the Community, Equipped for the Globe

Securing the full-time position meant more than a new title and a salary. It meant becoming a trusted node in the network of Cheyenne’s resilience. I am no longer an observer; I am a participant. When a storm hits, I’m part of the response ecosystem that includes firefighters, contractors, and city officials. The trust I built during my internship—showing up on time, listening more than I spoke, following through—was the currency that bought my place here.

The wind in Cheyenne still tells its stories. But now, I understand the language. It speaks of a climate in flux, of an economy on edge, of people trying to protect what they’ve built. My journey from intern to adjuster was a journey into the heart of these modern complexities. This desk, this city, this role—it’s where the abstract global headlines become tangible, one claim, one conversation, one repaired roof at a time. The work is challenging, often emotionally draining, but it is unequivocally real. And on the high plains, where the sky is vast and the storms are honest, there’s no place I’d rather be to meet the future, head-on.
